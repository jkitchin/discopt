* Potential Energy Surface Crossings (MECP / MECI)
:PROPERTIES:
:ARTICLE_TYPE: concept
:SOURCE_KEYS: yarkony1993 bearpark1994 harvey1998 levine2008 koppel1984 atchity1991 worth2004 lykhin2016 berkolaiko2021 kitchin2026
:STATUS: draft
:CREATED: 2026-08-22
:END:

Two electronic states of a molecule generally have different energies at a given
nuclear geometry, but along some geometries they become degenerate. The set of
degenerate geometries is the *crossing seam*, and its lowest-energy point is the
*minimum-energy crossing point* (MECP) or, when the two states have the same spin
multiplicity, the *minimum-energy conical intersection* (MECI). These points play
the role in spin-forbidden and photochemical reactions that a transition state
plays in ordinary thermal chemistry: in nonadiabatic transition state theory the
rate depends on the crossing energy through a Boltzmann factor citep:lykhin2016,
so the crossing point that matters is the *lowest* one, not merely a nearby one.

For an optimizer the problem is deceptively small. Let \(E_1(x)\) and \(E_2(x)\)
be two adiabatic electronic energies as functions of nuclear geometry \(x\). Then

\[ \min_x E_1(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad E_1(x) - E_2(x) = 0, \quad x \in [x^L, x^U] \]

is a small, dense, continuous, nonconvex NLP with one or two nonlinear equality
constraints — the shape a spatial branch-and-bound solver handles natively (see
[[file:../methods/spatial-branch-and-bound.org][Spatial Branch and Bound]]). What makes the class interesting is not its size but
three structural features, each of which changes what an algorithm can promise.

** Two cases, distinguished by whether the states can couple

If the two states differ in spin multiplicity — a singlet/triplet intersystem
crossing — the interstate coupling vanishes by spin symmetry in the
nonrelativistic Hamiltonian. Degeneracy is then a single condition, the seam has
dimension \(3N-7\), and the adiabatic surfaces *are* the diabatic ones, so
\(\min E_1\) subject to \(E_1 = E_2\) is the exact problem with no reformulation
needed. This is the case usually called MECP (or MESX, minimum energy seam of
crossing). Spin-orbit coupling, which actually drives the hop, is treated
perturbatively at the located point rather than being part of the geometry
optimization.

If the two states share a multiplicity, degeneracy requires *two* conditions:
the energies must match and the interstate coupling \(H_{12}\) must vanish. The
intersection space then has dimension \(3N-8\) and the two remaining directions
form the *branching space*, in which the surfaces separate linearly and trace out
the double cone that gives the conical intersection its name citep:atchity1991.
This is the MECI case, and it dominates photochemistry citep:worth2004.

That the degeneracy of a real symmetric matrix is a codimension-2 condition is
the von Neumann–Wigner theorem; the chemistry statement and the linear-algebra
statement are the same statement. Stripped of chemistry, locating an MECI is the
problem of finding parameter values at which a parametric real symmetric matrix
family has a double eigenvalue citep:berkolaiko2021.

** Why the surfaces are hard exactly where the answer is

Near an intersection the two adiabats are the eigenvalues of a \(2\times 2\)
potential matrix,

\[ E_\pm = \frac{W_{11}+W_{22}}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{W_{11}-W_{22}}{2}\right)^2 + W_{12}^2} \]

so the radical vanishes *at the solution* and \(E_\pm\) are non-differentiable to
any order precisely there. Every established algorithm in the field is built to
avoid differentiating through the cone; see [[file:../methods/crossing-point-optimization.org][Crossing Point Optimization]]. The
modern answer is to work with quantities that stay smooth through the
intersection — a pseudodiabatic surrogate citep:galvan2023, or the coefficients
of the characteristic polynomial of the potential matrix citep:richings2023 — and
that turns out to be exactly the reformulation a factorable global solver wants
(see [[file:../methods/eigenvalue-degeneracy-constraints.org][Eigenvalue Degeneracy Constraints]]).

The linear vibronic coupling model citep:koppel1984 is the canonical analytic
example: two harmonic diabats coupled linearly through a symmetry-breaking mode,
whose MECI is available in closed form and therefore serves as an oracle for any
proposed method.

** Three structural difficulties for a certified method

*Black-box surfaces.* \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) come from an electronic-structure
program. There is no algebraic expression to relax, so deterministic global
optimization is impossible on the *true* surfaces; any certified result is
necessarily a statement about a surrogate, and turning it into a statement about
the true surface requires a validated error bound. This is the same barrier the
molecular-conformation global-optimization literature met citep:maranas1994jcp,
and it is where the surrogate-relaxation methods discussed in
[[file:neural-network-embedding.org][Neural Network Embedding]] become load-bearing rather than optional.

*Redundant coordinates.* Production codes work in \(3N\) Cartesians and project
out the six translation and rotation modes. For a local optimizer this redundancy
is harmless. For branch-and-bound it is not: every translation and rotation of a
crossing point is also a crossing point, so the problem carries a six-parameter
continuum of equivalent optima on top of a coordinate count three times larger
than necessary. Internal coordinates or a gauge-fixed Cartesian frame are a
practical requirement.

*Permutation symmetry.* Molecules with equivalent atoms have permutationally
equivalent crossing points, so every seam basin appears once per equivalent
labelling — the same symmetry that motivates permutationally invariant polynomial
surrogates citep:braams2009 multiplies the search space for an exhaustive method.

** Why the global question is the interesting one

Multiple crossing points between the same pair of states are the norm rather than
the exception, which is exactly why automated *exploration* methods exist
citep:maeda2009addf,lindner2019. None of them certifies that the lowest one has
been found. Because the crossing energy enters a rate constant exponentially, the
difference between the lowest crossing point and the second-lowest is not a
slightly worse answer but a rate wrong by orders of magnitude: a 0.28 eV basin
error is a factor of \(\exp(0.278/0.02585) \approx 4.6\times 10^4\) at 300 K.

This is the argument for treating MECP location as a global optimization problem
with a certificate rather than as a local geometry optimization, and it is the
gap that a relaxation-based solver is positioned to close — subject to the
dimensional ceiling that spatial branch-and-bound imposes.
