The algorithms quantum chemistry uses to locate minimum-energy crossing points
(see [[file:../concepts/potential-energy-surface-crossings.org][Potential Energy Surface Crossings]]) divide into three families: local methods
that converge to *a* crossing point, exploration methods that generate many
candidates stochastically or heuristically, and a small mathematical literature
on the underlying double-eigenvalue problem. **No member of any of the three
returns a bound.** That absence is the opening for a relaxation-based global
solver, and it is worth being precise about, because it is the claim on which the
whole case rests.

** Local methods

*Lagrange–Newton* citep:yarkony1993 writes the KKT system of
\(\min E_1\) subject to \(E_1 - E_2 = 0\) (plus \(H_{12}=0\) for a same-spin
intersection) and applies Newton's method to it. It needs the interstate coupling
vector, which not every electronic-structure method can supply, and it inherits
Newton's local convergence basin.

*Gradient projection* citep:bearpark1994 — the Bearpark–Robb–Schlegel method, the
field's workhorse — forms a composite step from two orthogonal pieces: a
gap-reducing component inside the branching space, and the energy gradient
projected onto the intersection space. It also requires the coupling vector.

*The effective-gradient method* citep:harvey1998 specializes this to spin
crossings, where the coupling vanishes by symmetry and the branching space
collapses to the single direction \(\nabla(E_1-E_2)\). This is the standard
MECP method for intersystem crossing and needs no coupling vector.

*Branching-plane updating* citep:maeda2010 removes the coupling-vector
requirement for same-spin intersections by replacing the second branching
direction with one updated iteratively from the optimization history.

*The smooth penalty method* citep:levine2008 removes both the coupling vector and
the constraints, minimizing

\[ F_\sigma(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}(E_1 + E_2) + \sigma\,\frac{(\Delta E)^2}{|\Delta E| + \alpha} \]

with \(\sigma\) escalated across a sequence of unconstrained minimizations. Its
practical popularity is deserved — it needs only energies and gradients — but it
is the single worst formulation to hand a relaxation-based solver, for reasons
developed in [[file:eigenvalue-degeneracy-constraints.org][Eigenvalue Degeneracy Constraints]]. The rule of thumb that follows is
worth stating in one line: *the penalty form is right for a local or
derivative-free search and wrong for branch-and-bound.*

Method development continues on the electronic-structure side rather than the
optimization side — e.g. MECI optimization at the DFT/MRCI(2) level
citep:wang2025dftmrci2 — but the optimizer underneath remains one of the above.

** Exploration methods

Because a molecule typically has many crossing points, automated exploration
matters more in practice than the local step does.

*ADDF seam mapping* citep:maeda2009addf follows anharmonic downward distortions
to map minimal energy points on seams of crossing systematically, and the
approach is folded into the *SC-AFIR / GRRM* global reaction route mapping
machinery citep:maeda2018grrm17, which explores MESX and MECI structures as part
of building a whole reaction network. This is arguably the closest thing the field
has to a systematic global search — but it is a heuristic exploration strategy,
not an exhaustive one, and it certifies nothing.

*Multistate metadynamics* citep:lindner2019 (metaFALCON) biases dynamics along a
gap collective variable to sample the seam. Stochastic; converges in distribution,
not to a proof.

*Bayesian optimization* citep:somaki2025 has been applied to searching for the
global minimum and the most stable conical intersection together. This is the
closest the chemistry literature comes to a *global* claim, and it is worth
reading carefully: a GP posterior supplies a probabilistic statement about an
unexplored region under modelling assumptions, not a valid lower bound on the
objective. It does not certify.

** The mathematical problem underneath

Locating an MECI is the problem of finding parameters at which a parametric real
symmetric matrix family acquires a double eigenvalue. citep:berkolaiko2021 give a
simple iterative scheme for two-parameter families with proven *quadratic*
convergence, extended to Hermitian families (three parameters) and to triple
eigenvalues (five parameters). It is a local root-finder — fast, elegant, and
still silent on whether the located degeneracy is the lowest one. Its value here
is diagnostic: it shows that the difficulty of the global question is not the
degeneracy condition, which is well understood, but the *search*.

** Surrogate constructions built to be smooth through the cone

Two recent constructions exist because the adiabats are non-differentiable at the
answer, and both are directly useful to a global solver:

- A *pseudodiabatic three-surface model* citep:galvan2023 fits smooth surrogate
  quantities whose eigenvalues reproduce the adiabats.
- The *characteristic-polynomial* approach citep:richings2023 machine-learns the
  trace and determinant of the potential matrix instead of its eigenvalues, since
  the symmetric functions of the eigenvalues stay smooth through the intersection.

The second is the more consequential for optimization: it produces exactly the
polynomial quantities a factorable relaxation wants. It also carries a soundness
caveat that the chemistry framing does not surface, discussed in
[[file:eigenvalue-degeneracy-constraints.org][Eigenvalue Degeneracy Constraints]].

** What none of them provides

Every method above answers "here is a crossing point". None answers "no crossing
point in this region is lower than \(L\)". The distinction is the entire content
of a dual bound, and it is what a spatial branch-and-bound method
([[file:spatial-branch-and-bound.org][Spatial Branch and Bound]]) supplies on a factorable surrogate. The precedent for
certified answers on a potential energy surface is the molecular-conformation
work of the αBB school citep:maranas1994jcp — the crossing-point problem is the
same bargain (certificate in exchange for a dimensional ceiling and a surrogate),
applied to a different critical point.
