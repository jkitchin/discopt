A minimum-energy crossing point asks for the lowest point of a set defined by an
*eigenvalue degeneracy* (see [[file:../concepts/potential-energy-surface-crossings.org][Potential Energy Surface Crossings]]). Every algebraic
way of writing that condition defines the same feasible set, so a local optimizer
is nearly indifferent between them. A relaxation-based global solver is not:
measured on one two-mode conical-intersection model with an analytic answer, the
formulations span roughly four orders of magnitude in solve cost, and the
formulation the field standardized on is the one that fails outright. This
article is the formulation map, plus the open items a state-of-the-art
implementation would have to settle.

** The formulations, in order of relaxation quality

Let \(W(x)\) be the \(2\times 2\) symmetric potential matrix with entries
\(W_{11}, W_{22}, W_{12}\), and \(E_\pm\) its eigenvalues.

*** Diabatic form — degeneracy as \(W = cI\)

Degeneracy of a \(2\times 2\) real symmetric matrix is exactly
\(W_{11}=W_{22}\) **and** \(W_{12}=0\), i.e. \(W\) is a multiple of the identity:

\[ \min \tfrac{1}{2}(W_{11}+W_{22}) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad W_{11}-W_{22}=0,\; W_{12}=0 \]

No radical, no squaring, and the constraints inherit whatever structure the
diabats have. On a linear vibronic coupling model citep:koppel1984 the two
constraints are *affine* and the objective is a convex quadratic, so the problem
is a convex QP and a solver disposes of it at the root with zero branching. That
is a property of the LVC model rather than of diabatic representations in
general, and any comparison that uses it as the baseline should say so.

The catch is practical: it requires a diabatic representation, which is not
uniquely defined and not what an electronic-structure code hands you.

*** Difference form — the spin-crossing case

When the interstate coupling vanishes by spin symmetry the adiabats *are* the
diabats, and the whole problem is

\[ \min W_1 \quad\text{s.t.}\quad W_1 - W_2 = 0. \]

A linear difference of two surfaces relaxes as well as the surfaces themselves.
This is the cheapest correct formulation whenever it applies, and measurements
consistently favour it over every reformulation below — including the
characteristic-polynomial form, which on a spin crossing is a *bad* trade because
squaring the surfaces widens the relaxation for no structural gain.

*** Characteristic-polynomial (discriminant) form — representation-free

The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are the symmetric functions of
the eigenvalues and stay smooth through the intersection even though the
eigenvalues do not citep:richings2023:

\[ T = E_1+E_2 = W_{11}+W_{22}, \qquad D = E_1E_2 = W_{11}W_{22}-W_{12}^2 \]

Degeneracy is the vanishing of the discriminant \(T^2-4D=(E_1-E_2)^2\), and at
degeneracy \(E_1=E_2=T/2\):

\[ \min \tfrac{1}{2}T \quad\text{s.t.}\quad T^2 - 4D \le 0. \]

The inequality is exact because \(T^2-4D \ge 0\) identically. This needs no
diabatization and is exactly the pair of quantities the machine-learned-seam
literature already fits — the reason it is the natural bridge between a learned
surrogate and a global solver.

Two properties deserve emphasis, one favourable and one a trap.

**Favourable: LICQ fails, and that is fine here.** Since \(T^2-4D=(E_1-E_2)^2\),
its gradient \(2(E_1-E_2)\nabla(E_1-E_2)\) vanishes at *every* feasible point.
Constraint qualification fails on the entire feasible set, so Newton-type local
methods lose their convergence theory. Measured consequences are
algorithm-dependent rather than universal — an SQP implementation can fail on
roughly half of random starts while a trust-region interior method handles the
degeneracy completely — but a relaxation-based solver does not need LICQ at all.
It needs valid bounds. This is one of the few places where a global method is
structurally *better suited* than the local alternative rather than merely more
expensive.

**The trap: the inequality form is only exact for a genuine symmetric matrix.**
\(T^2-4D\ge 0\) holds identically when \(T\) and \(D\) are the trace and
determinant of a real symmetric \(W\). It does *not* hold when \(\hat T\) and
\(\hat D\) are two independently fitted surrogates — which is precisely what
citep:richings2023 constructs. Wherever the fit has \(\hat T^2 < 4\hat D\), the
constraint \(\hat T^2-4\hat D \le 0\) admits an open region of points that are not
crossings at all, and a solver will certify a global optimum of the wrong problem
without any warning. A state-of-the-art implementation must either (a) use the
equality \(\hat T^2-4\hat D=0\), (b) constrain the fit so the discriminant is
nonnegative by construction (e.g. fit \(T\) and the gap-squared, or fit the
matrix entries rather than the invariants), or (c) verify \(\hat T^2\ge 4\hat D\)
over the box before relaxing. This is a soundness issue in the *formulation*, not
in the solver, and it is not visible from the chemistry framing.

*** Adiabatic form — the radical

\[ \min E_- \quad\text{s.t.}\quad E_+ - E_- = 0 \]

Expressible in any factorable solver with =sqrt=, and it does solve. It is
strictly worse than the forms above because the radical's argument vanishes
exactly at the solution: the envelope of \(\sqrt{\cdot}\) on a box touching zero
is one-sided, since the endpoint tangent there has infinite slope. A
general-purpose engine that silently drops that facet loses the tightest part of
the envelope in exactly the region that contains the answer. The same shape
affects \(\arcsin/\arccos\) at \(\pm 1\), \(\mathrm{arcosh}\) at \(1\), and
\(\log\) near zero — the fix is to place the tangent at an interior point rather
than dropping it, which is a *bound-changing* change and needs the corresponding
verification discipline.

*** Penalty form — do not

\[ \min \tfrac{1}{2}(E_1+E_2) + \sigma\frac{(\Delta E)^2}{|\Delta E| + \alpha} \]

The standard local method citep:levine2008 in unconstrained form. As an object to
relax it is close to worst case: a squared numerator over a denominator whose
lower bound is the small smoothing parameter \(\alpha\), so the relaxation of the
quotient is enormous and the dual bound is useless — measured relative gaps of
\(10^3\)–\(10^4\) on *two-variable* problems that the constrained form certifies
in five nodes. The one place it is correct is a derivative-free search
(e.g. DIRECT), which requires an unconstrained problem and returns no bound
anyway.

** Coordinates and gauge

Free Cartesian coordinates are the second formulation mistake. Beyond tripling
the variable count, every translation and rotation of a crossing point is also a
crossing point, so the problem carries a six-parameter continuum of equivalent
optima. Measured behaviour on a triatomic: internal coordinates certify in five
nodes and a second; the same energies in nine free Cartesians reached a 93 %
gap after three minutes; gauge fixing (atom 0 at the origin, atom 1 on \(+x\),
atom 2 in the \(xy\) half-plane) restores certifiability at roughly 66× the nodes
of the internal-coordinate form, because the energies then reach the coordinates
through \(\sqrt{\sum(\Delta x)^2}\) compositions rather than directly.

The practical rule — *internal coordinates or a gauge-fixed frame, never free
Cartesians* — is well supported. The *mechanism* is not yet isolated: dimension
(3 → 9), the extra =sqrt= compositions, and the degenerate optimal manifold are
confounded in that measurement, and a nine-coordinate internal-coordinate
instance also fails to certify in the same budget. Separating them needs a
controlled experiment — fix translation only, then rotation only, then both —
which has not been run. A degenerate continuum of optima does not by itself break
convergence of spatial branch-and-bound in *value*; what it reliably does is
prevent fathoming by strict improvement.

** Beyond two states

The chemically interesting question is one level up: which *pair* of several
low-lying states crosses lowest. That is a disjunction, and it makes the problem
a genuine MINLP or GDP (see [[file:../concepts/generalized-disjunctive-programming.org][Generalized Disjunctive Programming]]):

\[ \min_{x,y} E \;\;\text{s.t.}\;\; y_p = 1 \Rightarrow \big(W_i(x)=W_j(x),\; E=W_i(x)\big),\quad \textstyle\sum_p y_p = 1 \]

Measured at three states, enumeration of the three pairs beats the disjunctive
formulation by about 2.7× — the expected answer at that size, where the
alternative is three tiny independent problems. The disjunctive form is the
interesting one as the number of states \(K\) grows, since pairs grow as
\(O(K^2)\) and a single tree can prune an entire pair on a bound instead of
solving it; note that pairs which never cross inside the box are exactly the case
enumeration handles worst (it spends a full solve proving infeasibility) and a
disjunctive formulation handles best. Where the crossover lies is unmeasured, and
is the obvious next experiment. Big-M constants for such a formulation should
come from interval bounds, not from a sampled maximum — a too-tight \(M\) cuts
off valid crossings silently.

*Two routes to a \(K\)-state degeneracy condition*, with different relaxation
behaviour:

1. *Discriminant.* Degeneracy of a \(K\times K\) symmetric matrix is the
   vanishing of the discriminant of its characteristic polynomial — a polynomial
   of degree \(2K-2\) in the coefficients. It generalizes cleanly on paper and
   degrades badly in practice: McCormick relaxations of a high-degree polynomial
   are very weak, so this is likely unusable beyond \(K=3\).
2. *Eigenvector lifting (proposed, untested).* Introduce the degenerate
   eigenvalue \(c\) and two eigenvectors explicitly: find \(x, c, u, v\) with
   \(W(x)u = cu\), \(W(x)v = cv\), \(u^\top v = 0\), \(\|u\|=\|v\|=1\). Every
   condition is *bilinear* in the lifted variables, which is the class McCormick
   handles natively — a far lower-degree object than the discriminant, at the
   cost of \(2K+1\) extra variables. The hazard is immediate and is the same one
   as free Cartesians: \((u,v)\) is determined only up to a rotation within the
   degenerate eigenspace, so the lifting *manufactures* a continuum of equivalent
   optima and must be gauge-fixed (e.g. pin the largest component of \(u\), or
   impose a normalization plus a sign convention). Worth measuring before
   believing; the analogy to the Cartesian result suggests the gauge fix is not
   optional.

** Symmetry

Molecules with equivalent atoms have permutationally equivalent crossing points,
so each seam basin appears once per equivalent labelling and a branch-and-bound
tree carries every symmetric copy. This is the classical symmetry problem in
mathematical programming (orbital branching, isomorphism pruning, symmetry-
breaking constraints), and it is untouched in this domain. Permutationally
invariant polynomial surrogates citep:braams2009 are the chemistry-side answer to
the *fitting* problem and are worth examining as a formulation-side answer to the
optimization problem: a surrogate written in invariants has the symmetry quotiented
out before the solver ever sees it.

** The surrogate certification gap

Because real surfaces are black boxes, a certificate on a fitted surrogate
certifies the global optimum *of the fit*. Closing the remaining gap rigorously
requires a validated error bound \(|E_\text{true}-E_\text{surrogate}| \le
\epsilon\), which brackets the true crossing point by optimizing the surrogate
\(\pm\epsilon\). Rigorous-remainder machinery (Taylor and Chebyshev models)
exists for *factorable* functions but not for a data-fitted surrogate; the
data-driven Lipschitz-informed underestimator line citep:ravutla2026 is the
closest current approach to a bound derived from samples.

The relaxation quality of the surrogate itself is the other half. Reduced-space
McCormick relaxation of a program citep:mitsos2009mccormick is the foundation;
its application to trained networks citep:schweidtmann2019ann and to Gaussian
processes citep:schweidtmann2021gp is the state of the art for embedding a fitted
model in a deterministic global solve, and the strong MIP formulations for
piecewise-linear networks citep:anderson2020 are the ReLU-side counterpart (see
[[file:../concepts/neural-network-embedding.org][Neural Network Embedding]] and [[file:../comparisons/maingo-vs-discopt.org][MAiNGO vs discopt]]). Measured in-repo, a pair of
small tanh networks over three coordinates gave a dual bound of \(-20.6\) against
an optimum near \(1.6\) after ten minutes — the modelling is right, the
certificate is out of reach at that width — so this is the item that would change
what is *possible* rather than what is convenient.

** Summary of what is settled and what is not

| Question | Status |
|---|---|
| Which formulation for a spin crossing | Settled: the plain difference form |
| Which formulation for a same-spin intersection | Settled: diabatic if available, else characteristic polynomial |
| Penalty objective in branch-and-bound | Settled: never |
| Coordinates | Settled in practice: internal or gauge-fixed; mechanism unmeasured |
| Discriminant inequality on a *fitted* \((T,D)\) | Unsound without an extra guard; not addressed in the source literature |
| \(K\)-state degeneracy | Open: discriminant degrades, eigenvector lifting untested |
| Symmetry | Open and unmeasured |
| Certified answer on a learned surface | Open; the main research item |
